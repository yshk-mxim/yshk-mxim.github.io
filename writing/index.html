  • The Flood and the Trust: The Risk of AI Omissions

    Aug 25, 2026·LinkedIn·4 min read·Trust & Safety

    A year after a frontier model estimated driving distances as straight lines across fjords, its successor got the same job: plan a trip, find the tickets, check everything.   • Why an AI Agent Is Not a Team Player

    Aug 11, 2026·LinkedIn·3 min read·Agents in Practice

    Klein et al. published ten challenges for making automation a team player in 2004. Graded against that rubric as a quarterly performance review, a frontier Claude Opus agent fails every one that matters: it holds no model of the work the organization is doing, cannot negotiate interdependent decisions, cannot own an outcome, and cannot carry a supervisor's feedback into its next task. A layout designer and a plumber who each iterate perfectly within their own discipline still oscillate between two incompatible house designs. The evidence is a real 29-day, 59-hour build (174 files, 2,416 tests, ruff/mypy/pytest/Hypothesis/Playwright/security scanning) that shipped, late and over budget, with twelve decisions implemented correctly and specified badly enough that the product did not do what it was for. As a tool the same agent aces the review. Don't hire agents as employees; give employees agentic tools.

  • AI Will Not Displace Human Labor in the Near Term. And It Will Make the Work Better

    Aug 6, 2026·LinkedIn·5 min read·Work & Economics

    GenAI is strongest at fluency, the repetitive work with well-specified and cheaply checkable completion, and weakest at judgment: deciding what the task is and whether the result is right. That fluent, checkable work was largely automated last century by office software and factory machines, and aggregate employment did not fall. What is left is 'just figure it out.' Where the pattern fits, gains arrive immediately: 14% more support issues resolved per hour, 40% faster short writing, 55.8% faster on a defined coding task. Where it doesn't, fluency becomes sabotage, a volume of under-defined work nobody can verify. Software got its harnesses only because experts spent years and heavy budget building them for themselves; humanoid robotics faces the same judgment gap plus real-time adaptation, and cannot run agentic loops when falling down costs more than a failed compile. Companion piece to the SSRN paper.

  • Adding Trust to Untrustworthy AI

  • Why AI Can Generate Text Yet Cannot Write

    Jul 15, 2026·LinkedIn·3 min read·Using AI Well

    A frontier model set to extra thinking, wired to best-in-class NLP tools, a graded style rulebook, and a section-diversifying pipeline still produced text with no soul, while a memo written by hand in ten minutes scored higher and read as human. The reason is mechanical: an LLM maps previous tokens onto the next token, but a human maps intent onto a written piece, and that pre-verbal intent never appears in the training data because it forms as you write. Flower and Hayes named the missing step, translating, decades ago. RLHF only records preferences over finished outputs. The human in the editing loop supplies the missing signal one document at a time, which is why the most expensive component of the system is still a person.
